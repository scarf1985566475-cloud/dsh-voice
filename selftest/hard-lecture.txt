Good morning. Today we are going to work through the third variant of normative ethics, which is usually called virtue ethics. This is a tradition that was established by Aristotle in ancient Greek philosophy, and it has been developed in many different flavours and specifications since then. Roughly speaking, virtue ethics sits somewhere between deontology and consequentialism. It holds that there are certain principles of value, but that those principles are constantly negotiated in the real world through habit, through ontological learning, and through the communities we inhabit. The central question is not what should I do, but what kind of person should I become. Aristotle argued that excellence of character is a settled disposition, acquired by practice rather than by instruction, and that the virtuous person perceives the salient features of a situation directly rather than by calculation. Later thinkers, including Aquinas and MacIntyre, extended the account in quite different directions. Critics point out that the theory can be circular, that it presupposes a shared conception of flourishing, and that it offers little guidance when two virtues conflict. Defenders reply that this is a feature rather than a defect, because moral life is messier than any decision procedure admits.
